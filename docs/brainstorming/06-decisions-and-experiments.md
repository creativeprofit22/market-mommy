# Recommendations and experiments

## Make advice testable

The underlying recommendation record can be detailed while the user initially sees only the next action and reason.

Useful fields:

1. Recommended action and intended customer.
2. Problem and timing.
3. Why it fits the user's skills, access, budget, and time.
4. Supporting evidence and counterevidence.
5. How it could produce money.
6. Economic assumptions and plausible ranges, where defensible.
7. Smallest useful experiment.
8. Success criteria and stop conditions.
9. What would change the recommendation.
10. Outcome and subsequent learning.

Keep commercial value, evidence strength, personal fit, delivery feasibility, and time to cash distinct. A sorting score is not a probability of success.

## Practical experiments

Examples include customer conversations, a narrowly scoped paid pilot, testing a clear offer with suitable existing contacts, and measuring delivery time before committing to a larger offer.

Distinguish levels of evidence: positive comments, stated interest, an agreed next step, a paid commitment, money received, successful delivery, and repeat purchase are different outcomes.

Record experiments before results arrive to reduce hindsight bias. Failed tests can improve decisions, but do not label them commercial success.

### Hypothetical example

A person with relevant skills considers a reporting-automation service for a narrow agency segment.

Evidence might include independent descriptions of manual reporting work. Counterevidence might include preference for bundled tools and resistance to another supplier.

A useful test could be a fixed-scope paid pilot offered to suitable, approved contacts. Measure paid acceptance, delivery hours, achieved benefit, and margin. Stop or revise if interest never becomes payment or delivery costs erase the margin.

This is an illustrative pattern, not an actual researched opportunity or income claim.

## Simulations that earn their cost

| Method | Practical use |
| --- | --- |
| Simple income-and-cost scenarios | Explain customer count, price, hours, and expenses |
| Funnel scenarios | Explore outcomes under plausible response and purchase assumptions |
| Capacity scenarios | Check whether delivery remains feasible if sales increase |
| Pricing sensitivity | Show how conversion and margin assumptions affect results |
| Retention scenarios | Compare acquiring customers with keeping existing ones |
| Stress tests | Check whether a decision survives worse costs or demand |
| Historical replay | Evaluate a policy using only then-available information |
| Evidence-grounded objection review | Identify assumptions to test with real people |

Expose assumptions, their sources, uncertainty, and relevant correlations. If one unsupported input controls the result, recommend gathering evidence rather than generating more scenarios.

Agentic objection review may use documented concern categories to challenge an offer. It must not claim that synthetic personas reproduce a market. “Eight simulated buyers said yes” does not establish an 80% conversion rate. Multi-agent agreement is not independent validation.

For early users, a transparent calculator may be more valuable than an elaborate simulation.

## Social strategy

Social content is optional, not a mandatory step for every business. Recommend it only when it helps the user reach suitable buyers at a sensible cost.

A useful pattern is:

**Observed buyer problem → original evidence → useful claim or demonstration → relevant offer → measured outcome.**

Possible formats include myth-versus-measurement tests, workflow teardowns, honest before/after demonstrations, evidence-backed contrarian analysis, small original datasets, and documented experiments.

“Disruptive” should mean useful and distinctive, not deceptive outrage. No fabricated reviews, claims, or engagement.

Track qualified attention and resulting business, not reach alone. Preserve uncertainty about attribution. Publishing and contact actions remain approval-gated.

## Outcome learning

Track which evidence informed which action, under which conditions, and what happened. Include price, costs, delivery time, sales cycle, objections, and repeat business where available.

Do not automatically generalize one user's outcome to every business. Keep private customer information isolated and reuse learning only with appropriate permission and sufficient context.
