# Business stages and journeys

Business stages describe the help someone needs. They are not subscription plans, rigid identities, or necessarily labels shown to users. A successful freelancer launching a new service may need first-customer guidance for that offer.

## Stage map

| Working stage | User's question | Desired outcome |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Finding a direction | I want to start, but what should I do? | Choose a realistic idea worth testing |
| Getting the first customer | I have an idea, but nobody is paying | Make a clear offer and test it with real buyers |
| Making sales consistent | I have earned money, but it is unpredictable | Find a repeatable way to attract and win customers |
| Growing without overwhelm | It works, but I am stretched thin | Improve pricing, delivery, repeat business, and profitability |
| Managing complexity | We need better decisions across teams and markets | Support advanced monitoring, forecasting, and coordination |

Initial focus: the first two stages, with a simple path into the third. The exact stage model remains a proposed design rather than a finalized taxonomy.

## Finding a direction

### Learn enough about the person

Ask progressively about:

- Skills, work experience, and things they could realistically learn.
- People and communities they can already reach.
- Available hours and how soon they need income.
- Money they can afford to spend or lose.
- Preferred work and activities they want to avoid.
- Geographic, practical, and qualification constraints.

Allow “I'm not sure.” Do not demand a business strategy from someone who needs help forming one.

### Provide a small shortlist

For each direction explain:

- What they would sell and who might pay.
- Why people might need it, with evidence and limitations.
- Why it fits this person's situation.
- What would make it difficult.
- What it would cost to try.
- The smallest useful demand check.

Recommend one direction with an explanation, while allowing correction. Balance demand, ability to deliver, access to buyers, time, and money.

A service based on existing skills may be more practical for near-term income than building software. Evaluate this case by case; do not turn it into a universal rule.

## Getting the first customer

Help define:

1. Who the offer is for.
2. The problem it solves.
3. What the customer actually receives.
4. A starting price or price range with explicit assumptions.
5. Where suitable buyers can be reached appropriately.

Possible outputs include an offer description, a small test, customer-conversation questions, messages for human review, and a simple record of responses and purchases.

Avoid premature busywork. A full website, broad content schedule, or complex automation may be unnecessary before testing demand. Requirements such as permits, qualifications, insurance, and safe delivery still take precedence where relevant.

## Making sales more consistent

Shift from possibilities to observed performance:

- Where did recent customers come from?
- Why did they choose the offer?
- Which work leaves the most money after relevant costs?
- What do customers buy again?
- Where do interested people lose interest?
- How much delivery time does each sale require?

Recommendations may include focusing on a stronger customer segment, simplifying an offer, adjusting pricing, asking for referrals, adding genuinely useful repeat work, or stopping a channel that generates attention without sales.

## Progress and transitions

Useful progress includes suitable customer conversations, objections learned, offers made, paid commitments, money received, delivery hours, and costs.

Ideas generated, research hours, and posts published are activity measures—not proof of business progress. Rejection can teach something useful without being represented as revenue.

Do not automatically advance someone based on a single sale or a vanity metric. Stage changes should reflect their current problem, observed outcomes, and their own correction of the tool's understanding.

## Income expectations

“Good money” depends on the person, location, costs, hours, and time horizon. Clarify it rather than assuming a universal number.

Show transparent scenarios when useful: a stated price and customer count imply a certain amount of revenue before explicitly listed costs and taxes. Do not frame scenarios as earnings predictions or guarantees.
