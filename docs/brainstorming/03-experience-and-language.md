# Experience and language

## Accepted direction

Use plain, respectful language by default. Business terminology may be useful in context, with explanation. Developer language should not appear in the ordinary user experience.

Someone can be expert at their craft and unfamiliar with business terminology. Simple language should not mean childish language or reduced analytical quality.

## Proposed primary experience

The home screen should answer:

1. **Your next step:** one clear action, with an honest effort estimate when available.
2. **Why this is worth doing:** a short explanation tied to the person and evidence.
3. **What happened:** an easy way to record results so guidance can change.

Illustrative copy, not a claim about an actual user:

> Next: speak to three suitable business owners.
>
> We found signs that this problem matters, but we do not yet know whether people will pay you to solve it. These conversations will help you decide whether to continue.

Possible controls: “Help me do this,” “Show me the evidence,” and “This doesn't fit my situation.”

Chat supports the journey; it should not be the entire product. Beginners should not have to invent the right prompt.

## Progressive explanation

- **Recommendation:** what to try.
- **Reason:** why it fits this situation.
- **Detail:** evidence, assumptions, calculations, limitations, and optional terminology.

Keep advanced views available when useful, without making them mandatory. Adapt language to the task and expressed preference, not assumptions about intelligence.

## Wording guide

| Avoid by default | Prefer |
| --- | --- |
| Ideal customer profile | Who is most likely to buy |
| Validate your hypothesis | Check whether people want this |
| Value proposition | Why someone would choose you |
| Go-to-market strategy | How you will find your first customers |
| Conversion rate | How many interested people become customers |
| Customer acquisition cost | What it costs to win a customer |
| Unit economics | What you keep from each sale after its costs |
| Churn | Customers leaving or not coming back |
| Agent orchestration | Do not expose the machinery |
| Scraping pipeline | Sources we check |
| Confidence score | Explain how strong the evidence is and why |
| Run a simulation | Explore what could happen |

These are contextual examples, not mechanical substitutions. Do not label an amount “profit” if important expenses are excluded.

## Uncertainty and errors

Prefer specific limitations:

> We found repeated requests, but no reliable evidence of what buyers will pay.

Avoid unexplained percentages that imply measured certainty.

Translate technical failures into their effect on the decision:

> We could not check this source today. This recommendation uses the other sources listed below.

Distinguish no evidence, stale evidence, missing coverage, and failed collection. Never hide a material limitation behind reassuring wording.

## Keep backstage details backstage

Users should not need to understand agents, models, tokens, command lines, connectors, databases, or infrastructure errors. Technical details can exist in developer documentation and appropriate diagnostic surfaces without entering the core journey.

User-facing explanations should still identify relevant sources, dates, costs, external actions, and approval requirements. Hiding implementation complexity must not hide consequential behavior.

## Avoid manipulative guidance

No guaranteed-income claims, fabricated testimonials, false urgency, or pressure to spend money to “unlock success.” Acknowledge when an idea is a poor fit or the evidence does not justify further investment.
